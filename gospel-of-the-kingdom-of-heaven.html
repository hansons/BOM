The Gospel of the Kingdom of Heaven

Matthew’s five great discourses, the life told between them, and the people and commands around them

“And he went throughout all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues and proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom and healing every disease and every affliction among the people.” Matt 4:23

Matthew names the message three times: “the gospel of the kingdom” (4:23; 9:35; 24:14). The first two, nearly word for word alike, frame chapters 5 to 9: teaching and preaching in the Sermon on the Mount, healing in the mighty works that follow. Every passage on this sheet links to its ESV text.

“Our Brother” is a description, not a title the Gospels use. The idea is theirs: he calls those who do the Father’s will his brothers (Matt 12:50), and after the resurrection sends word to “my brothers” (Matt 28:10).

The passion and the commission

Matthew 26:2 to 28:20

    “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you.     The last words of the Gospel send the disciples back to the five discourses: “all that I have commanded you” is what the book has just recorded.

    The women who followed

    Mary called Magdalene, Joanna the wife of Chuza, Susanna, “and many others, who provided for them out of their means.” Luke 8:1–3

    They followed him from Galilee to the cross, and were first at the empty tomb. Matt 27:55–56; 28:1–10

    The seventy-two

    Sent ahead of him two by two into every town and place he was about to visit, with instructions much like the Twelve’s. Luke 10:1–20

    John the Baptist’s own messengers carried questions to Jesus and his answer back. Matt 11:2–6; Luke 7:18–23

    The mixed multitude

    “And great crowds followed him from Galilee and the Decapolis, and from Jerusalem and Judea, and from beyond the Jordan.” Matt 4:25

    About the five books

    Matthew ends five long blocks of teaching with nearly the same sentence (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1), and each block follows a stretch of narrative. Read that way, the Gospel falls into five books, framed by the birth stories at the start and the passion at the end. The five discourses are widely recognized. B. W. Bacon’s further proposal, that they deliberately mirror the five books of Moses, is still debated.

    Chapter 23 is spoken to the crowds in the temple and chapters 24 and 25 to the disciples on the Mount of Olives, so some readers count only 24 and 25 as the fifth discourse. This sheet follows the wider reading.
